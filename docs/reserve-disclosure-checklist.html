12 questions before you trust a reserve claim

A dashboard or a PDF answers “what are the holdings”. A buyer needs to answer a harder question: “can I verify this without trusting the page?” These are the questions that separate a screenshot from evidence.

The checklist

  1. As-of time per line. Is every reserve line dated, or only the total? A single date on the cover hides stale prices inside.
  2. Source per line. Does each line name the statement, file, or API it came from — not just “custodian”?
  3. Haircut per asset. Is a Treasury bill valued at face, at market, or at a haircut? Is the haircut stated per line?
  4. Missing price behaviour. If one line has no price, does the report fail closed, or does it quietly treat the line as zero?
  5. Liabilities on the same date. Is token supply or fund liabilities measured at the same timestamp as the assets?
  6. Named owner of the data. Is there one person or role who signed off on the inputs, or is it “the team”?
  7. Recompute without the issuer. Can a buyer recompute NAV from the file alone, without your server or dashboard?
  8. Signature and key. Is the report signed? Which key? Can the buyer verify it offline with a published public key?
  9. What is deliberately hidden. Does the report say what was left out — wallets, counterparties, strategy — and why?
  10. Change since last report. Is there a version, a diff, or at least a date and a reason for every material change?
  11. Failure path. What happens when a source is late, a price is stale, or a custodian statement does not reconcile?
  12. What it is not. Does the report say plainly that it is not a statutory audit, not legal advice, and not a solvency guarantee?

What good looks like

A signed JSON file with one row per reserve line, a source hash, a timestamp, a haircut, and a verdict. The buyer runs one command, sees signature: valid and verdict: backed (or underbacked / incomplete), and can recompute NAV without asking the issuer for a spreadsheet.

If a line is missing a price, the verdict is incomplete and the ratio is null. That is the point: the report refuses to invent a number.

Where most disclosures stop

  • A PDF with a total and a logo
  • A dashboard that only the issuer can query
  • A monthly attestation with no line-level source
  • A “backed 1:1” claim with no liabilities on the same date

A dashboard is not evidence. A signature over a file the buyer can keep is.

Use it on your own disclosure

If you are an issuer, run these 12 questions against your current reserve page. If three or more have no answer, a buyer will ask them for you — usually at the worst moment.
